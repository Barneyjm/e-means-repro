Beyond the paper

Follow-up experiments on top of the reproduction in the README. None of this is from the e-means paper; the ideas tested are old ones from the vector-quantization, neural-codebook and statistics literature, and prior art is named where I found it. Everything ran on one Apple M2 Max (GPU through PyTorch, 12 CPU cores) on 2026-11-10/11. Raw logs and full tables are in results/beyond/; scripts are in experiments/.

1. Summary

Two questions. Does the recipe (update per batch, forgetting running totals, a big-then-small step curve, timed repair, a final clean-up, settings from formulas) carry over to other classic algorithms? And are there old ideas the recipe leaves out that would add to it?

Where the recipe carried over

Algorithm Result Verdict
k-means for search indexes (6 datasets) 3 passes ≈ 25 Lloyd passes at small k, 4–9% less scanning at k = 16,384; 49% less on New York Times embeddings, where 21% of the rows are exact copies (section 14). One case (text embeddings, k = 16,384) where it is 9% worse. works, with one exception
Product quantization 3 passes beat 100 Lloyd passes once the authors' low-dimension batch rule is used works
Gaussian mixtures CPU estimator: 250× faster than scikit-learn at 1M rows for the same score; needs k-means++ seeds on well-separated clusters works
NMF 3 passes ≈ 34–79 full passes; within 0.1% of scikit-learn's NMF in 4% of the time works
Hidden Markov models 1 pass ≈ 40–80 Baum-Welch passes, but only when the formulas are fed the number of symbols; the step curve, revival and clean-up add nothing works after re-mapping
Recommender ALS nothing with the formulas; with a hand-picked batch and step, 5× less time to the same error, and the gain is partly regularization partly
Topic models (LDA) the published step is 4–8× too large; tuned, it ties the 2010 online rule no real gain
PageRank only "use fresh values" applies, which is Gauss-Seidel does not apply

The pattern: the recipe pays where the full-pass method needs many passes and gets stuck (k-means, mixtures, hidden Markov models, NMF). It pays little where the full-pass method already converges in ten passes (LDA, ALS) or where there is no noise to average (PageRank). The fitted formulas are specific to k-means: outside it, the batch size and step had to be re-chosen in every case but NMF and mixtures.

Old ideas added to the recipe

  • A dozen ideas tested on k-means. All but one did nothing, hurt, or gave no more than the same time spent on an extra pass (section 4). The paper's own choices held up.
  • The exception, a cluster-size penalty from 1988, helps search indexes on some data and hurts on other data. Rechecked with the authors' code in real Faiss indexes (section 14): roughly 3–10% fewer vectors scanned on SIFT, news and landmark embeddings at the larger k, nothing on question embeddings, and on Last.fm at k = 4,096 it gives back most of what plain e-means gains. On the New York Times dataset it saves 60–80%, but that comes from 146 groups of duplicate rows that are the nearest neighbour of 29% of the queries; with the copies removed there is no saving. It is in the estimator as EMeans(balance=0.3), off by default; measure before using it.
  • A rule for the penalty's strength predicts the best value on three datasets, is wrong on one and cannot be fitted on another.
  • For mixtures, two annealing ideas made things worse. For hidden Markov models, growing the state count did.

  • Held-out error stops improving at four passes (about −0.4%). Training error keeps falling, and search cost follows the training error, because the database vectors of an index are the training vectors.
  • The curve is bumpy because the published formula doubles the batch size at 3, 6 and 15 passes; the first pass count on a larger batch (6, 15) is no better than the one before it.
  • More Lloyd passes do nothing: 50 and 100 passes are within 0.2% of 25. Lloyd has converged to a worse solution, and e-means is past it from three passes on.
  • Twenty-five e-means passes cost less time than 25 Lloyd passes (15.7 s against 18.9 s) and give an index that scans 8% less.
  • At k = 4,096 (one seed) the curve flattens sooner: every count from 5 to 25 passes is 1–3% below Lloyd 25, and so are 50 and 100 Lloyd passes. On GloVe at k = 4,096 (5 seeds) it keeps going: −1.4% ± 2.2 at three passes, −3.1% at five, −5.8% ± 2.2 at ten.

At 15 and 25 passes the formula caps the step at 1. Clusters a batch does not touch are then left as they are, as in the authors' code (section 12).

What the plain recipe does (columns "e-means 3" and "e-means 5"):

  • On image descriptors, raw images and word vectors, three passes are within about ±3% of 25 Lloyd passes at small k and 4–9% better at k = 16,384. Five passes are at least as good as Lloyd on all of those.
  • On the 1,536-dimension text embeddings it depends on k. At 4,096 there is no real difference (±2%), at one seventh of the training time (5.1 s against 36.5 s). At 16,384 (3 seeds) the three-pass index is 9% worse than Lloyd's, because its lists are less even (imbalance 1.40 against 1.31); with the faded size penalty it is level with Lloyd's (−1.4% ± 2.1), at 17.8 s of training against 139 s.
  • On the New York Times embeddings the difference is large: the index from three passes scans 49% fewer vectors than the Lloyd index at k = 4,096. That file has many exact duplicate rows (section 14). Lloyd from a random start ends with more than half of its 4,096 clusters holding fewer than 10 vectors and a few holding over a thousand (imbalance 7.4, where 1.0 is perfectly even); a k-means++ start and 100 passes do not fix it. The revival step in e-means is what helps here (imbalance 2.1).
  • One pass is 4–13% worse than Lloyd on five of the six datasets, as on SIFT.
  • A k-means++ start for Lloyd made the index worse on Fashion-MNIST (by 5–11% against a random start), as it did for e-means on SIFT in section 4.

4. Old ideas the recipe does not use

The recipe has five ingredients. The older literature has many more that the paper does not combine with them. I added them to the GPU implementation as switches (experiments/emeans_lab.py, which with all switches off reproduces emeans_torch.py bit for bit) and ran each one alone on top of 3-pass e-means (a dozen ideas, some in two or three variants). 5. A size penalty

What it is

When a vector picks its centroid, busy centroids are handicapped:

assign x to the j that minimises |x − c_j|² + λ · D · (k · n_j / n − 1)

where n_j / n is cluster j's running share of the data (e-means already keeps it), D is the running mean squared distance, and λ is a strength with no units. Training takes about 2–4% longer.

This is the assignment step for the objective "clustering error + (λ·D/2) × imbalance", where imbalance = k · Σ (n_j/n)² (1.0 when all clusters are the same size). Imbalance is exactly what an inverted-file index pays for uneven lists: with one list probed per query, the expected number of vectors scanned is (n/k) × imbalance. So λ is an exchange rate between clustering error and list balance.

Prior art. The penalty is DeSieno's "conscience" for competitive learning (1988). The same linear size penalty is used for search indexes as balanced k-means: Liu et al. 2018 (FCFC), SPANN 2021, Microsoft SPTAG, Lance, and MicroNN 2025 in mini-batch form. Published results are mixed: SPANN reports a gain, while Tavenard, Jégou & Amsaleg 2011 and an Elastic engineering post report that balancing hurt. What I did not find elsewhere: the penalty combined with the e-means running totals, a fade-out version that ends as plain k-means, a strength expressed in units of the running error, and a rule for that strength. Those absences come from one literature search, not a proof.

Two versions, same strength λ:

  • Faded: λ follows the cosine curve down to zero, so training ends as plain k-means.
  • Held: λ stays to the end.

For scale, on SIFT at k = 16,384 no number of plain passes reached this: plain e-means levels off at −0.45% against Lloyd after 25 passes, and three passes with the faded penalty are at about −0.6%. The effect shows up where there are few vectors per cluster. A plausible reason, not tested: in high dimensions the best quantizers have nearly equal-sized cells (Gersho 1979), k-means gets stuck in solutions that are less even than that, and the penalty steers away from them early on.

How strong? A rule, and where it fails

Scan cost at a fixed recall depends on two things the training controls: how even the lists are and how good the centroids are. Fit, per dataset and k, over runs that use no penalty (different pass counts, revival thresholds, batch sizes):

log(scan cost) = a · log(imbalance) + b · log(training error) + constant

Raising λ moves along a trade-off curve whose slope is d(error)/d(imbalance) = −λ·D/2. Cost is lowest where the two effects cancel:

λ* = 2a / (b · imbalance)

7. Gaussian mixtures

The README has the GPU experiment: the recipe applied to EM for diagonal-covariance mixtures matches 40–57 passes of ordinary EM in three. Two additions.

8. Topic models (LDA) and NMF

LDA: the recipe as published does not transfer

Variational LDA already has a well-known online form (Hoffman, Blei & Bach 2010), and it is the recipe's update with a different step rule: a decayed running total of topic-word counts. So the test is narrower here: do the cosine step curve, the batch and step formulas, a repair step for dead or duplicate topics, and a final exact clean-up beat Hoffman's rule? experiments/lda_torch.py, validated against scikit-learn's batch LDA (perplexity equal to 0.0001% over 10 passes from the same start).

UCI New York Times bag of words, 100,000 documents, 8,000-word vocabulary, 100 topics, 3 seeds. Held-out perplexity by document completion on 10,000 documents; lower is better.

Hoffman's rule + the repair step 2152 – 2087 ± 15 – –
  • With the published formulas the step is 4–8× too large for LDA: the result gets worse after the first pass.
  • With the step divided by four, the recipe ties Hoffman's rule at 1–3 passes and is about 1% better at 5–10. Three online passes (22 s) match 5–7 full-batch passes (52–69 s).
  • Full-batch EM is not slow on this problem. It reaches its best value in 10–15 passes and then overfits, so there is much less to gain than with k-means.
  • The repair step helps a little (10 points at 3 passes, on Hoffman's rule too). The final clean-up does nothing.
  • Library defaults run in the same code are far behind Hoffman's best setting: gensim's settings 2259, scikit-learn's (batch 128) 2667 at one pass (1 seed each).
  • The best batch and step lie on a line of constant memory, batch ÷ step ≈ 33,000 documents (a third of the corpus); the formula's choice is about 4,700. No new formula was fitted.

experiments/als_torch.py: alternating least squares for explicit ratings, and an online version in the recipe's shape: mini-batches of users solved exactly and never stored (like assignments in k-means), decayed per-item normal-equation totals, items re-solved each batch, cosine step, last-pass clean-up. 11. PageRank

A short CPU study on the web-Google graph (875,713 pages, 5.1M links, damping 0.85), sweeps and seconds to a residual of 1e-6 / 1e-10 (experiments/pagerank_sweeps.py):

Method Sweeps Seconds
Power iteration (update everything from the previous sweep) 65 / 119 0.74 / 1.35
Gauss-Seidel (use each new value at once) 34 / 62 5.4 / 9.9 (slow only because of scipy's triangular solve)

Only one of the recipe's ingredients means anything here: update in pieces and use fresh values, which is the known Gauss-Seidel idea and halves the sweeps. PageRank is a deterministic fixed point with no noise to average away, so forgetting, a step curve, timed repair and a final clean-up either do nothing or slow it down.

12. My implementation against the authors' code

The authors' code is public (contrib/e_means in the Faiss repository). The Faiss release installed here (1.16.0) does not ship it, and I had written mine from the paper. I read theirs and compared; section 14 runs it. The main recipe matches: cosine step to 10% indexed by batch, revival every 5 updates below 0.1 × mean, donors drawn in proportion to mass without replacement, mass halved, alternating-sign split, last-pass mean from assignments recorded while the centroids move, one permutation per pass.

13. Caveats

  • One machine, one GPU type. GPU timings are training only and exclude data loading and scoring; CPU timings for the mixture estimator and the scikit-learn comparison shared the machine with GPU jobs.
  • Seeds: 2–5 per configuration for the search experiments, 3 for most others, 1 for some large runs (marked in the tables). Where the spread is not shown the number is one seed.
  • Search cost is exact-distance scanning inside the probed lists (IVF-Flat). No compressed vectors (IVF-PQ), no graph indexes, no re-ranking. Queries are held-out vectors from the same dataset, not real query logs.
  • The largest dataset is 1.1M vectors; k goes up to 16,384. Nothing here is at the paper's 100M–10B scale.
  • The size penalty was tuned on SIFT and then tried on five other datasets. It helped on three, was mixed on one (text embeddings: nothing at one k, 10% at another) and hurt slightly on one (GloVe). The recheck in section 14 adds one dataset where it clearly hurts (Last.fm) and shows the largest gain depends on duplicate rows. No dataset here tells you which case yours is; measure it.
  • The strength rule in section 5 is a two-factor model fitted per dataset. It was right on three datasets and wrong on one, and it needs pilot runs, so it is an explanation more than a tool.
  • Prior art for each idea comes from literature searches made the same night. Where I write that something was not found, that is the search coming up empty, not a claim of novelty.
  • Sections 2–13 compare my implementation only; section 14 is the only part that runs the authors' code.
  • The mixture, topic-model, hidden-Markov and factorization experiments each use one or two datasets.

What holds

  • Plain e-means, as the paper says. Three passes of the authors' code give indexes as cheap to search as 25 iterations of Faiss k-means on SIFT, CC-News, landmark and Yahoo, and much cheaper on NYT and Last.fm. It trains 5–8× faster on the same CPU (SIFT 1M, k = 16,384: 101 s against 816 s).
  • My estimator matches theirs with the split offset below: held-out error within 0.3% and search cost within about one seed spread on every row. It trains faster (SIFT: 12.5 s) because the two are built differently, not because the algorithm differs.

Split offset at revival

When a near-empty cluster is revived it takes half of a large one, and the two halves are pushed apart by a small relative offset. The paper gives ±2⁻⁷; the authors' code uses ±1e-4, and so does EMeans. On Last.fm at k = 4,096 the paper's value leaves 495 of 4,096 lists with fewer than 10 vectors and an index 120% dearer than Faiss k-means; ±1e-4 leaves 60 (authors' code: 51; seed 0). On NYT, CC-News and SIFT the two offsets give the same error and balance. Sections 2–11 used ±2⁻⁷; Last.fm is not used in them.

What does not hold: the size penalty as a general improvement

  • Small gains on three datasets. On SIFT, CC-News and landmark the penalty removes a further 3–10% of the distance computations at the larger k (authors' code; 2–9% with mine), less at k = 1,024.
  • Nothing on Yahoo Answers.
  • A loss on Last.fm at k = 4,096. Plain e-means is 36% cheaper than Faiss k-means there; with the penalty it is 3% cheaper to 6% dearer. The penalty does not even out the lists on this data: imbalance goes from 3.1 to 4.0–4.3, and the largest list holds 4,561 vectors without it and 8,138 (held) or 3,404 (faded) with it (seed 0). At k = 1,024 the held penalty is neutral with the authors' code and helps with mine.
  • The New York Times result is about duplicate rows. 53,047 of the 250,000 rows (21%) are in 20,985 groups of exact copies, the largest of 204. Faiss k-means does poorly on this file: about 2,400 of its 4,096 lists end up with fewer than 10 vectors and the rest carry the load (imbalance 7.34). Remove the copies (217,938 rows left) and Faiss k-means is no longer uneven: imbalance 1.56, against 1.67 for the authors' e-means and 1.12 with the penalty.

So on the copy-free file Faiss k-means is the cheapest or tied, plain e-means is 40–55% dearer, and the penalty only brings e-means back toward Faiss k-means. With the penalty most seeds do not reach 85% at all.

Why the copies matter: 146 groups have ten or more copies. They hold 1% of the rows, and one of them is the true nearest neighbour for 29% of the queries (queries that are not themselves copies of anything). Recall on those queries decides the whole comparison; on all other queries every index below is at 93–98%. Share of queries for which the index returns a row at the true nearest distance (seed 0):

Index Probes Queries whose nearest row has 10+ copies All other queries without a copy
Authors' + penalty held, file as distributed 32 / 128 49% / 97% 93% / 96%
Authors' + penalty held, copies removed 32 / 128 10% / 40% 94% / 96%
Faiss k-means, file as distributed 32 / 128 67% / 100% 94% / 98%
Faiss k-means, copies removed 32 / 128 10% / 37% 94% / 97%

With the copies in, each such group counts as ten to two hundred points when its list's centroid is computed, which pulls the centroid onto it; that is the likely reason those lists are probed early. With the copies out, the same row is one point in an ordinary list and is found late or never. The file is the benchmark as distributed, and real collections do contain duplicates, so the original result is not wrong. But it is a result about a handful of heavily duplicated rows, not about clumpy data in general, and I found no second dataset that shows it.

What to take from this

  • For building a Faiss IVF index, the authors' e-means with its defaults is what I would use.
  • Faiss k-means does poorly on a file with many exact copies; e-means' revival step repairs most of that, and the size penalty the rest. Removing the copies first also repairs it.
  • The size penalty is worth a trial where lists come out uneven for reasons other than dense clumps, and it is worth about 10% at large k on SIFT-like data. It is not a default.
