e-means-repro

An independent check of the paper "e-means: outperforming k-means (in one-tenth the epochs)" (Bessa, Mazaré, Szilvasy, Touvron, Picard, Douze, Jégou; arXiv 2611.09501, Nov 2026), plus a version of the algorithm that drops into scikit-learn, and follow-up experiments on whether the same recipe helps search indexes, product quantization, Gaussian mixtures, topic models, hidden Markov models and recommender factorization. The longer follow-up write-up is EXTENSIONS.md. Both are also on the project site, https://barneyjm.github.io/e-means-repro/.

Everything here was written from the text of the paper. It is not the authors' code. Theirs is in the Faiss repository (contrib/e_means); the Faiss release installed here (1.16.0) does not ship it. I later read it and list the differences in EXTENSIONS.md, then ran it next to mine (section 14).

What the paper claims

k-means groups vectors into k clusters. The standard method (Lloyd's algorithm) passes over the whole dataset about 25 times. The paper says a mini-batch variant with a decaying running average, a cosine step schedule and a rule that re-seeds near-empty clusters reaches a better result in 2–3 passes.

What I measured

SIFT1M (1,000,000 vectors, 128 dimensions), k = 16,384, random initialization everywhere. "MSE" is the mean squared distance from a vector to its nearest centre; lower is better. "Test" uses the 10,000 SIFT query vectors, which the clustering never sees.

GPU (PyTorch on an Apple M2 Max), 3 seeds

Same distance kernel for both methods. Time is training only.

The paper reports −0.35% at 3 passes for this dataset and k (its Table 5, against Flash-KMeans at 25 iterations). I measured −0.37% against my own Lloyd baseline. The paper says 1.4–2.6 passes are enough to match 25 Lloyd iterations; here it took 2 passes on test error and 3 on training error.

Faiss's own CPU k-means, run as an outside reference for the baseline (25 iterations, no subsampling, seed 0), gave test 41,704 and train 38,256, which is within 0.02% of the Lloyd row above.

With re-seeding of near-empty clusters switched off (100,000 vectors, k = 1,024), 182 clusters stayed empty and the error was about 2% worse, which matches the direction and rough size of the paper's ablation.

So in plain numpy, three passes of e-means are 6–9× faster than scikit-learn's 25-iteration KMeans at a million vectors and end slightly better. scikit-learn's default k-means++ run to convergence is still a little better at 100k, at 46× the time.

On small data there is nothing to gain. On load_digits (1,797 × 64, k = 10) and 2-D blobs (10,000 points, k = 8), 10 seeds each, everything finishes in about 10 ms, and EMeans with random initialization lands in a poor local minimum about as often as KMeans does (mean MSE 2.03 vs 1.90 on the blobs, 658 vs 658 on digits). The paper says its batch-size and step-size formulas are not tuned for tiny or low-dimensional data. Use this for large n and large k.

Beyond the paper: does the recipe carry over?

The paper's main results are clustering error. Its Appendix L also reports search recall for IVF indexes on eleven other datasets (e-means reaches the recall of a converged k-means sooner); it does not report scan cost or list balance. These are my own follow-up experiments, not claims from the paper. Raw logs and full tables are in results/beyond/. All runs: Apple M2 Max GPU, random initialization, SIFT1M unless stated.

The three experiments below came first. A second, larger round is written up in EXTENSIONS.md:

  • Search indexes on six datasets, checked in a real Faiss index against Faiss's own k-means.
  • A dozen old ideas tried on top of the recipe. One helped on some data: a cluster-size penalty (EMeans(balance=0.3)), worth roughly 3–10% of search cost on SIFT, news and landmark embeddings, nothing on question embeddings, and a loss on Last.fm. Its one large result (60–80% on the New York Times dataset) comes from duplicate rows in that file.
  • The recipe applied to NMF and hidden Markov models (works), ALS (partly), LDA (no real gain), PageRank (does not apply).
  • OnlineGaussianMixture, a scikit-learn-style mixture estimator: about 250× faster than GaussianMixture at 1M rows.
  • More passes: gains shrink after three and are nearly flat after eight.
  • A comparison with the authors' released code (EXTENSIONS.md section 12), and both run side by side in real Faiss indexes (section 14). Plain e-means holds up there as the paper says.

1. Does a search index built on the centroids get better?

The usual reason to run k-means at this scale is a vector-search index (IVF): each database vector is filed under its nearest centroid, and a query scans only the nearest few lists. What matters is how many vectors you must scan to reach a given recall. I filed the 1M base vectors under each set of centroids and searched with 100,000 held-out vectors (the SIFT learn set, with exact neighbours recomputed). 5 seeds; change is against Lloyd 25, paired by seed.

  • Three passes give an index as good as 25 Lloyd iterations at k = 1,024 and 4,096, and one that scans 3–4% fewer vectors at k = 16,384 (6% fewer at five passes), for 5–8× less training time.
  • One pass is not enough: the index scans 5–12% more.
  • A clustering-error gap of a few tenths of a percent turns into a scan-cost gap about ten times larger.
  • Lloyd with 10 iterations (the Faiss default for index training) scans about 3% more than Lloyd 25 and takes longer than three e-means passes.
  • Check on the method: for one set of centroids a real faiss.IndexIVFFlat returned the same recall and the same number of distance computations as my calculation, at every setting tried (experiments/faiss_ivf_check.py).

Three passes beat Lloyd 25 here too (seed spread is under 0.1%), and ten passes equal Lloyd 100 on the 1M set. The result is not monotonic in the number of passes (5 is worse than 3 on 1M), because the formula changes the batch size with the pass count. Timings at this size are dominated by per-call overhead. The authors' code uses one eighth of this batch size in low dimension; with that rule three passes beat Lloyd 100 and every extra pass helps (EXTENSIONS.md).

3. The same recipe for Gaussian mixtures (EM)

k-means is the hard-assignment special case of fitting a Gaussian mixture. I applied the same five ingredients to EM for diagonal-covariance mixtures (experiments/gmm_torch.py): per-batch updates of decayed totals (weight, sum, sum of squares), the cosine step curve, revival of starved components every 5 batches, a final clean-up from the plain totals of the last pass, and the paper's batch-size and step-size formulas unchanged.

Score: mean log-likelihood per held-out vector (higher is better). 1M training vectors, 64 dimensions, 3 seeds. sift64 is SIFT reduced to 64 whitened PCA dimensions. synth and skewed are drawn from known 256-component mixtures (near-equal and very unequal component weights), so the best possible score is known. My full-pass EM matches scikit-learn's GaussianMixture to 5 decimals from the same start.

Revival is the ingredient that matters everywhere. The cosine curve helps in three of four cases. The last-pass clean-up matters only when component weights are very unequal.

Three further checks:

  • The paper's formulas, fitted for k-means, are close to the best settings but not on them. A grid on 100k vectors (results/beyond/gmm_grid_100k.md) shows a ridge where batch size and step size shrink together. A 4× smaller batch with a 4× smaller step, tested on 1M, was better at 2–10 passes for K = 1,024 (+0.02) and on synth at 3 passes (+0.09), mixed for sift64 K = 256 (+0.03 to −0.02), and 10–80% slower. I did not fit a new formula.
  • The textbook online-EM step rule is not the main problem; the missing revival is. Given small batches the textbook rule without revival is far worse (−0.2 to −3.0 on sift64, −0.4 to −4.4 on synth). With revival added and its best batch size it comes within 0.04 of the cosine recipe on sift64 at 2–4× the time, and beats the default recipe on synth (+0.17 at 5× the time).
  • scikit-learn with its usual settings (GaussianMixture, CPU, k-means start, to convergence; one seed, different hardware and precision, so the times are not a like-for-like speed comparison): sift64 K = 256 −80.871 after 65 passes and 526 s; synth −89.292 (160 s); skewed −87.514 (163 s). Three online passes from a random start score −80.877, −88.951 and −87.313.

What I did not test

  • Billion-vector runs, and the approximate (IVF) assignment the paper uses there.
  • The paper's GPU comparison against Flash-KMeans, or any k-means++ baseline at 1M.
  • The paper's own benchmark datasets other than SIFT (EXTENSIONS.md adds five more public sets for search).
  • The CPU numbers at 1M are one seed each.
  • An end-to-end index with compressed vectors (IVF-PQ), graph indexes, or real query logs.
  • Mixtures with full covariance matrices, a downstream task that uses the mixture, or more than 3 seeds.
  • Ordinary EM from a k-means start on the GPU (the scikit-learn row above is the only such run).

Use it

from emeans_sklearn import EMeans

km = EMeans(n_clusters=4096, n_epochs=3, random_state=0).fit(X)   # X: (n_samples, n_features) float32 or float64
km.cluster_centers_, km.labels_, km.inertia_
km.predict(X_new); km.transform(X_new); km.score(X_new)

EMeans(n_clusters=4096, balance=0.3)        # not in the paper: a size penalty; helps on some data, hurts on other (EXTENSIONS.md, sections 5 and 14)

from online_gmm_sklearn import OnlineGaussianMixture               # same recipe for diagonal Gaussian mixtures (section 7)
gm = OnlineGaussianMixture(n_components=256, n_epochs=3, random_state=0).fit(X)
gm.predict_proba(X_new); gm.score(X_new)

pip install -e . installs it (numpy, scipy, scikit-learn ≥ 1.6). It follows the scikit-learn estimator contract: it works in Pipeline, clone, GridSearchCV and passes scikit-learn's check_estimator suite (pytest: 54 passed, 1 skipped for EMeans; 89 passed, 2 skipped for OnlineGaussianMixture). Batch size and step size default to the paper's scaling laws (one eighth of the batch for 16 features or fewer, as in the authors' code) and can be overridden. Dense input only.

How it is built from standard parts:

  • Assignment (nearest centre): BLAS matrix multiply in cache-sized chunks, run across a thread pool. numpy releases the GIL in the multiply, subtract and argmax, so this scales across cores without compiled code.
  • Per-cluster sums: a scipy.sparse one-hot matrix times the batch.
  • Everything else (running averages, cosine schedule, re-seeding, final averaging) is a few lines of numpy.

The algorithm in brief

For each cluster keep a decayed count N and a decayed sum S; the centre is S / N. For each mini-batch:

  1. Assign each vector in the batch to its nearest centre.
  2. N ← (1 − α)·N + α·(batch count), S ← (1 − α)·S + α·(batch sum).
  3. α follows a cosine from α0 down to 0.1·α0 over the whole run.
  4. Every 5 batches, any cluster whose N is below 10% of the average takes half of a large cluster, picked with probability proportional to N. The two halves are nudged apart by ±1e-4 (the authors' code; the paper says ±2⁻⁷).
  5. At the end, each centre is replaced by the plain average of the vectors it won during the last pass.

Batch size and α0 come from the paper's Table 2: B = 0.035·k^0.13·n^0.88·T^0.81 (÷8 above 2M vectors, rounded to a power of two) and α0 = 0.824·k^−0.46·B^0.90·n^−0.45·T^−0.11.

Notes

  • Hardware: Apple M2 Max, 64 GB, 12 cores; PyTorch 2.15 (MPS), faiss-cpu 1.16.0, numpy 2.5, scipy 1.18.
  • The paper's MSE values are on a different scale from mine, so only relative gaps are compared.
  • A fused addmm distance kernel made the GPU code 3.8× faster than the broadcast form, with identical assignments. Half precision was another 13% faster but gave worse test error at 3 and 5 passes, so it is off.
  • results/v1_k16384.log times include scoring passes; use results/optimized_k16384.log for timings.
